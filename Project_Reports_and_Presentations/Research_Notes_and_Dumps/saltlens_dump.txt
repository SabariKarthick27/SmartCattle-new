P20 [style=None]: ABSTRACT
P21 [style=None]: Rainfall can rapidly dilute hypersaline brine in solar salt pans, but a weather forecast alone cannot establish how a managed pond will respond. This project presents SaltLens DSS, an implemented, evidence-gated decision-support prototype for salt-pan management. The system combines timestamped pan observations, weather records, a deterministic rainfall–dilution mass balance, versioned operator-configured rules, recorded operator decisions and an auditable field-validation workflow. It is built with a Next.js user interface, FastAPI services and MongoDB Atlas storage.
P22 [style=None]: The central design principle is an explicit boundary between what was observed, what was forecast, what a simplified physical model calculated, what a rule recommended, and what an operator decided. Each stage carries its own evidentiary status and none silently replaces another. A machine-learning evidence gate blocks predictive output until a genuine labelled dataset and an independently evaluated model exist; consequently no machine-learning accuracy figure is reported.
P23 [style=None]: A North Pan case in Salem, India, uses a notebook-sourced pre-event record of 250 g/L salinity and 20 cm depth on 13 March 2026 at 13:30 IST. The reported rainfall event began on 14 March at 23:30 IST, with 90 mm of rainfall reported. The rainfall-only replay predicts 172.41 g/L and 29 cm, whereas operator-reported post-event readings are 20 g/L and 50 cm. An outbound brine transfer of unknown volume occurred and the post-event readings lack independent corroboration, so the case is descriptive and cannot establish model accuracy. A separate September 2026, 25 mm forecast scenario predicts 222.22 g/L and demonstrates the versioned rule-based recommendation and a recorded operator acceptance, without a documented physical harvest.
P24 [style=None]: The prototype therefore demonstrates reproducible software behaviour, provenance control and audit-preserving reconciliation. It does not demonstrate generalised physical prediction, trained machine-learning performance or operational benefit; each of these requires separate empirical evidence, and the report specifies the studies needed to obtain it.
P25 [style=None]: Keywords: solar salt pans, digital twin, decision support, provenance, rainfall dilution, field reporting, audit trail

P154 [style=Heading 1]: CHAPTER 1
INTRODUCTION
P155 [style=Heading 2]: 1.1  Background
P156 [style=None]: Solar salt production is among the oldest industrial uses of solar energy. Seawater or sub-soil brine is admitted into a sequence of shallow, shaded-free ponds, called pans, where sunlight and wind evaporate water, the dissolved salts become progressively concentrated, and sodium chloride eventually crystallises and is harvested. The process is simple in principle, but the economics and the quality of the product depend on how closely the operator can control two state variables in every pan: the concentration of the brine, usually expressed in grams per litre (g/L), and the depth of the water column, usually expressed in centimetres (cm).
P157 [style=None]: Because the pans are open to the atmosphere, they are exposed to weather in a way that few other industrial processes are. Sunshine, temperature, humidity and wind drive evaporation and therefore drive production. Rainfall does the opposite. A single heavy shower adds freshwater to a pan that has taken days or weeks to concentrate, lowers the salinity of the brine, raises its depth, and can undo a large part of the concentration work. In regions with intense monsoon or cyclonic rainfall, such as the south-eastern coast of India, salt-pan operators therefore watch weather forecasts closely and take protective or corrective action when significant rain is expected.
P158 [style=None]: The operator's decisions are consequential. Brine can be moved to a storage or reservoir pan before the rain, pans can be drained, bunds can be raised or breached, a pan that is close to harvest salinity can be harvested early, and brine can be re-concentrated afterwards. Each of these actions changes the water and dissolved-salt inventory of the pan, and each has a cost. Acting too early wastes production time; acting too late loses brine and salt; acting on the wrong information can be worse than not acting at all.
P159 [style=Heading 2]: 1.2  Rainfall and Brine Management in Solar Salt Pans
P160 [style=None]: At the simplest physical level, rainfall onto a pan adds water without adding dissolved salt. If the brine is assumed to mix uniformly and no water or salt leaves or enters the pan by any other route, the dissolved-salt mass stays constant while the volume grows, so the concentration falls in inverse proportion to the depth. This rainfall-only mass balance is the simplest quantitative statement of the dilution problem, and it is the physical core of the system described in this report.
P161 [style=None]: Real pans are not this simple. Water can be transferred between pans, pumped out, drained through sluices, lost by seepage, gained by runoff from surrounding land, or lost by overflow when the bunds are overtopped. Rain may not mix uniformly with dense brine; a fresher layer can float on top of the brine for some time, so a reading taken near the surface can differ strongly from a reading taken near the bottom. Measurements themselves are uncertain, and they are often recorded by hand in notebooks, with approximate times and unclear sampling locations. The gap between a clean idealised calculation and a messy field outcome is exactly where a decision-support system can be either useful or misleading.
P162 [style=Heading 2]: 1.3  Motivation
P163 [style=None]: Weather forecasts are widely available, but a forecast is a statement about the atmosphere, not about a pond. A forecast of 25 mm of rainfall does not, by itself, say what the salinity of a particular pan will be after the event; that depends on the initial salinity, the initial depth, what the operator does in the meantime, and how the rainfall is actually distributed. A system that merely displays forecasts leaves the translation from weather to pan-level consequence to the operator's experience.
P164 [style=None]: At the other extreme, a system that presents a single predicted number, with no indication of whether it was measured, forecast, calculated or guessed, invites over-trust. A salinity of 172 g/L shown on a screen carries very different meanings if it was read from a refractometer, computed from a simplified equation, or produced by a trained model of unknown reliability. In an industrial decision-support setting, mistaking one for another can lead to wrong actions and, equally important, to wrong conclusions about whether the system works.
P165 [style=None]: The motivation for this project is therefore twofold. The first part is practical: to give a salt-pan operator a pan-specific view that combines recorded observations, weather information and a transparent physical calculation, and that suggests an action through a rule the operator can read and change. The second part is methodological: to build the system so that the evidentiary status of every figure and every decision is recorded and displayed, so that a software demonstration is never mistaken for a physical validation, and a recommendation is never mistaken for a completed action.
P166 [style=Heading 2]: 1.4  Problem Statement
P167 [style=None]: Rainfall can rapidly dilute hypersaline brine in solar salt pans, yet forecasts alone cannot establish the response of a managed pond. Existing practice relies on operator experience and paper records; existing digital approaches tend either to present weather data without pan-level interpretation, or to present model outputs without a record of what evidence supports them. There is a need for a decision-support prototype that (i) maintains a pan-specific, source-labelled state; (ii) computes a reproducible, hand-verifiable physical response to forecast rainfall; (iii) produces explainable, versioned, operator-configurable recommendations; (iv) records what the operator actually decided; and (v) records field outcomes and any later corrections without erasing the history of what was previously claimed.
P168 [style=Heading 2]: 1.5  Objectives
P169 [style=None]: The objectives of the project are the following.
P170 [style=List Paragraph]: To design and implement a pan-specific digital twin that assembles source-labelled observations and weather evidence, and that keeps observed, forecast, simulated, recommended and decided information visibly separate.
P171 [style=List Paragraph]: To implement a deterministic rainfall–dilution mass balance whose results can be verified by hand, and to document its assumptions and the conditions under which it does not apply.
P172 [style=List Paragraph]: To implement a versioned, operator-configured rule engine that records the compared variable, threshold, comparison direction, rule version and outcome of every recommendation.
P173 [style=List Paragraph]: To implement an evidence gate for machine learning that blocks predictive output until a genuine labelled dataset, a leakage-safe evaluation protocol and an independently evaluated model exist.
P174 [style=List Paragraph]: To record operator decisions as decisions, and to keep them distinct from physical action records.
P175 [style=List Paragraph]: To implement an auditable field-validation workflow that records pre- and post-event measurements with provenance, interventions, verification state, and append-only correction history, including controlled timestamp reconciliation.
P176 [style=List Paragraph]: To demonstrate the workflow on a real North Pan case from Salem, India, and on a separate forecast scenario, while stating precisely what each case does and does not establish.
P177 [style=List Paragraph]: To specify the empirical studies required before any claim of physical accuracy, machine-learning performance or operational benefit can legitimately be made.
P178 [style=Heading 2]: 1.6  Scope and Limitations
P179 [style=None]: The scope of this project is an implemented decision-support prototype and its documented evidence boundaries. The project is deliberately modest in what it claims. It includes a user interface, service layer and database, a rainfall-only mass balance, a rule engine, an operator-decision record, a field-event reconciliation module and exports that carry caveats. It does not include a trained machine-learning model, and no machine-learning accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, RMSE, MAE, calibration, risk score or SHAP result is reported.
P180 [style=None]: The field evidence is limited. The March North Pan case contains one rainfall event, a post-event state that was reported by the site operator without independent corroboration, and an outbound brine transfer whose volume is unknown. It is therefore a descriptive, intervention-affected case and not a statistical accuracy study. The separate September scenario is a forecast-to-decision demonstration; it uses a manually entered forecast and a separate observation, and it does not add a field-validation event. Finally, no controlled comparison against ordinary operator practice has been made, so operational benefit is not demonstrated.
P181 [style=Heading 2]: 1.7  Summary of Contributions
P182 [style=None]: The contribution of the project is not a more accurate salinity predictor. It is an explicit, implemented boundary between three things that are frequently conflated: a software-consistent forecast, a provenance-labelled field report, and an independently verified physical outcome. The specific contributions are listed below.
P183 [style=List Paragraph]: A pan-specific digital twin whose interface labels every figure as OBSERVED, FORECAST, SIMULATED, RECOMMENDED or DECIDED, so that a simulated state cannot silently replace a measured observation.
P184 [style=List Paragraph]: A deterministic rainfall-only mass balance with a worked, hand-verifiable calculation for each scenario, and an explicit list of the flows it excludes.
P185 [style=List Paragraph]: A versioned rule engine whose output retains the compared variable, threshold, version and outcome, and which states when no machine-learning estimate was read.
P186 [style=List Paragraph]: A machine-learning evidence gate that reports zero genuine labelled training rows and therefore withholds predictive output.
P187 [style=List Paragraph]: A field-event reconciliation workflow that corrects an unsupported seed timestamp while retaining the displaced value as superseded history, and that prevents an operator-reported timestamp from being promoted to instrument verification.
P188 [style=List Paragraph]: Exports that present UTC and IST representations and carry material caveats.
P189 [style=List Paragraph]: A claim-to-evidence discipline, applied throughout this report, that matches each claim to the evidence needed to support it.
P190 [style=Heading 2]: 1.8  Methodology Overview
P191 [style=None]: The project followed an iterative software-engineering approach. Requirements were first derived from the decision problem of a salt-pan operator and from the need to keep evidence categories separate. A three-tier architecture was then designed: a Next.js presentation tier, a FastAPI service tier that holds validation, calculation and reconciliation logic, and a MongoDB Atlas storage tier. The deterministic mass balance was derived and checked by hand for each scenario before being implemented, so that the software result could be compared against an independent manual calculation.
P192 [style=None]: The rule engine, the operator-decision record and the machine-learning gate were then added as separate components, each with explicit inputs and recorded outputs. The field-validation module was developed against a real North Pan event; during its development an unsupported seed-script timestamp was identified, traced and reconciled through a controlled workflow rather than overwritten. Throughout, developer-reported tests and build checks were used to support implementation testing, and screenshots of the running interface were retained as evidence of implemented behaviour.

P195 [style=Heading 1]: CHAPTER 2
LITERATURE REVIEW
P196 [style=Heading 2]: 2.1  Overview
P197 [style=None]: This chapter reviews the published work that is closest to the problem addressed by SaltLens DSS. The relevant literature is spread over several communities that rarely cite one another: agricultural water–salt management with digital twins, remote sensing of natural and industrial salt pans, hydrology of salt ponds, and machine-learning prediction of salinity in soils and rivers. Each addresses salinity, but in a different medium, with different observation regimes and different prediction targets. The purpose of the review is therefore not to rank the studies, but to identify what each documents, what it does not document, and where an operator-facing, evidence-tracked decision-support workflow for hypersaline pond brine can be positioned.
P198 [style=None]: The review is organised by theme. Sections 2.2 to 2.5 cover digital twins, remote sensing, hydrology and machine learning. Section 2.6 considers decision support, digital agriculture and explainability. Section 2.7 discusses provenance and evidence-graded reporting, which is the least developed theme in the salt-pan literature and the one on which this project concentrates. Sections 2.8 and 2.9 summarise the comparison and state the research gap.
P199 [style=Heading 2]: 2.2  Digital Twins for Water–Salt Management
P200 [style=None]: A digital twin is a continuously updated digital representation of a physical asset that is used to monitor, simulate and support decisions about that asset. In agriculture, digital twins have been proposed for irrigation and drainage control. Qin et al. describe a digital-twin-enabled intelligent irrigation–drainage system for precision water–salt management in saline agroecosystems [1]. The work is relevant to salt-pan management because it treats water and salt as coupled quantities that must be managed together, and because it frames the digital twin as the layer that connects sensed state to control decisions.
P201 [style=None]: Broader reviews place digital twins within precision agriculture and smart farming [14], and within infrastructure and climate-resilience settings [8]. These works discuss architectures in which sensors, models and decision modules are connected, and they emphasise the potential of such systems to support climate adaptation. They are, however, mostly concerned with the architecture and potential of digital twins rather than with the evidentiary status of the individual values a twin displays. A twin that mixes measured, forecast and simulated values without labelling them can appear authoritative while concealing which parts of its state were ever observed.
P202 [style=None]: Digital agriculture more generally is discussed in the overview by Balasundram et al., which considers the role of digital technologies in mitigating climate change and supporting food security [9]. Such overviews provide context for why decision-support tools are increasingly applied to weather-exposed production systems, including the salt pans that are the subject of this project.
P203 [style=Heading 2]: 2.3  Remote Sensing of Salt Pans
P204 [style=None]: Remote sensing has been used to characterise natural salt pans. Schauer et al. investigate the prediction of inundation and desiccation states of salt pans in the Western Pannonian Basin using remote sensing, groundwater and meteorological data [7]. Their work is directly relevant to the present project because it links meteorological drivers, such as rainfall, to the wetting and drying of salt pans, and because it shows that weather records can be treated as explanatory inputs to a salt-pan state. The target of that work is the state of a natural pan observed from space; it is not the salinity and depth of a managed industrial pond under operator control.
P205 [style=None]: Lemenkova applies machine-learning methods to remote sensing data in order to map salt-pan crust dynamics in the Sebkha de Ndrhamcha, Mauritania [11]. The study demonstrates that crust and surface properties of salt pans can be mapped from imagery. The emphasis is again on mapping surface conditions rather than on supporting a pond-level decision about rainfall response, and the observation regime is satellite imagery rather than in-pond salinity and depth measurements.
P206 [style=None]: Taken together, the remote-sensing studies establish that salt-pan state responds to meteorological conditions and can be monitored at scale. They do not provide an operator with a pan-level record that distinguishes the operator's own reading from a calculation or a recommendation.
P207 [style=Heading 2]: 2.4  Salt-Pond Hydrology and Hypersaline Dynamics
P208 [style=None]: Salt-pond hydrology studies provide the physical context for changing salinity. Jarecki and Walkey report on the variable hydrology and salinity of salt ponds in the British Virgin Islands [17], documenting that pond salinity varies substantially over time in response to hydrological inputs and losses. Shen et al. study salt dynamics in coastal marshes and the formation of hypersaline zones [10], which illustrates how evaporation and water exchange concentrate salts in shallow systems.
P209 [style=None]: These studies support the physical premise of this project: that rainfall, evaporation and water exchange jointly determine pond salinity, and that a pond-level calculation must make its assumptions about those flows explicit. They also show why a single rainfall-only calculation is an idealisation. Real ponds have inflows, outflows, seepage and non-uniform mixing, and the salinity reading at one location may not represent the whole pond.
P210 [style=None]: In the soil context, Wang et al. review soil salinisation in drylands and the measurement, monitoring and management of it [13]. Although the medium differs, the review reinforces a point that is central to this project: salinity assessment depends on measurement method and sampling location, and a number is only as meaningful as the measurement that produced it.
P211 [style=Heading 2]: 2.5  Machine-Learning Studies of Salinity
P212 [style=None]: A substantial body of work applies machine learning to salinity prediction. Jiang et al. simulate soil salinity dynamics, cotton yield and evapotranspiration under drip irrigation using ensemble machine learning [6]. Wang et al. review the quantitative assessment of soil salinity using remote sensing and machine-learning algorithms [12]. Melesse et al. predict river-water salinity using hybrid machine-learning models [15]. Mohan et al. integrate artificial intelligence and explainable AI for precision crop-yield prediction [16].
P213 [style=None]: These studies show that data-driven models can be effective when a suitable labelled dataset exists, and they illustrate the standard concerns of such work: feature selection, validation design and interpretability. However, they concern different media (soil and river water), different observation regimes (remote sensing, monitoring stations, field trials) and different prediction targets (salinity indices, yield, river salinity). Their reported performance cannot serve as a numerical benchmark for hypersaline pond-brine events unless a common dataset and matched outcomes are available. Citing their accuracy figures as a baseline for a salt-pan system would be a category error, and this report does not do so.
P214 [style=None]: This observation motivates the machine-learning evidence gate of SaltLens. If a system accepts a trained model's output into an operator-facing screen before a labelled dataset and an independent evaluation exist, it risks presenting an unvalidated estimate as a result. The gate in SaltLens withholds such output and reports the absence of genuine labelled training data explicitly.
P215 [style=Heading 2]: 2.6  Decision Support, Explainability and Weather Sensitivity
P216 [style=None]: Decision-support systems in agriculture are increasingly paired with explainable AI. Abou Samra and Ali review explainable artificial intelligence in smart agriculture with a focus on interpretable remote sensing for sustainable decision-making [4]. The common theme is that decision makers need to understand why a system recommends an action. For a salt-pan operator, an explanation that names the compared variable, the threshold, the comparison direction and the rule version is more actionable than an opaque score.
P217 [style=None]: Weather is a recurring operational constraint in agriculture. Mastronardi et al. evaluate anti-rain agricultural nets, examining structural parameters and functional efficiency [3], which exemplifies physical protection against rainfall in crop systems. Studies in unrelated domains, such as artificial-intelligence-based strain screening [2] and the effect of adverse weather on automated vehicles [5], illustrate a common need to state the operating conditions under which an automated system's output is valid. These works are cited only for that general point; they are not salt-pan studies, and no performance comparison with them is made.
P218 [style=Heading 2]: 2.7  Provenance and Evidence-Graded Reporting
P219 [style=None]: Provenance, the record of where a piece of information came from and how it was transformed, is a mature topic in data management but is rarely applied explicitly to salt-pan decision support. In the salt-pan setting, the practical difficulties are easy to list. Observations are often handwritten in notebooks. Times are approximate. The same pan may be described by different names. A system might auto-generate seed values during development that are later mistaken for field records. An outcome might be affected by an intervention that was never logged. None of these problems is solved by a more accurate model; they are solved by recording the source, the status and the history of every value.
P220 [style=None]: An evidence-graded workflow separates at least five kinds of information. Observed values are readings recorded against a pan or site. Forecast values are statements about weather that has not occurred. Simulated values are computed from stated equations and inputs. Recommended actions are outputs of a written rule. Decided actions are what a person actually decided, which is a record of a decision and not a figure. SaltLens adds a sixth, the reported field outcome, which carries its own verification state. The literature on digital twins and machine learning surveyed above does not, in the sources reviewed here, define an operator-facing workflow with exactly this separation, and this is the gap on which this project concentrates.

P225 [style=Heading 2]: 2.9  Research Gap
P226 [style=None]: The review shows that digital twins, remote sensing, hydrology and machine learning each contribute relevant knowledge, but that none of the reviewed work centres on the traceable handoff between an observation, a scenario, a versioned rule, a human response and reconciled field evidence for an individual managed pan. Three gaps follow.
P227 [style=List Paragraph]: Evidence status gap. Twins and models are described at the level of architecture and prediction performance, but the evidentiary status of individual displayed values is not treated as a first-class design element.
P228 [style=List Paragraph]: Field-outcome gap. Field outcomes that are affected by interventions, such as brine transfers of unknown volume, are difficult to use as validation data. A workflow is needed that keeps such events visible, labelled and excluded from quantitative validation, rather than silently imputing them or reporting them as accuracy statistics.
P229 [style=List Paragraph]: Recommendation-versus-action gap. Recommendations are commonly evaluated as if they were outcomes. A decision-support system needs to keep the recommendation, the operator's decision and the physical action as separate records so that its effect can be evaluated even when an operator rejects the suggestion.
P230 [style=None]: SaltLens addresses these gaps at the software-engineering level. Its contribution is a complementary, implemented workflow, not a superior salinity predictor.
P231 [style=Heading 2]: 2.10  Summary
P232 [style=None]: The literature supports the physical premises of the project, that rainfall dilutes brine and that pond salinity responds to hydrological inputs and losses, and it supplies the vocabulary of digital twins, decision support and explainability. It does not supply a numerical benchmark for hypersaline pond-brine events, and it does not define the evidence-labelled operator workflow that this project implements. The next chapter surveys the techniques currently available for managing rainfall in salt pans and states how the proposed approach addresses their limitations.
P233 [style=Heading 1]: CHAPTER 3
EXISTING TECHNIQUES AND THE PROPOSED APPROACH
P234 [style=Heading 2]: 3.1  Introduction
P235 [style=None]: This chapter describes the techniques that can be, or are typically, used to manage rainfall risk in solar salt pans, summarises the advantages and disadvantages of each, and explains how the approach taken in SaltLens DSS addresses the disadvantages. The description of traditional practice is necessarily general: practices differ between salt works, and the aim here is to identify the structural strengths and weaknesses of each class of technique rather than to evaluate a particular operator.
P236 [style=Heading 2]: 3.2  Experience-Based Operation
P237 [style=None]: In many salt works, decisions about rainfall are made by experienced operators who read the sky, track regional weather forecasts and know the behaviour of each pan. The advantages of this approach are considerable. The operator knows the local conditions, can respond immediately, requires no infrastructure, and can integrate information that no formal system captures, such as the feel of the brine or the condition of a bund.
P238 [style=None]: The disadvantages are equally clear. The knowledge is tacit and resides in individuals, so it is difficult to transfer, audit or improve systematically. Decisions are rarely recorded together with the information on which they were based, so it is hard to learn from a good or bad outcome. Quantitative reasoning about how much a given rainfall will dilute a given pan is approximate. And the approach scales poorly when the number of pans and the number of operators grow.
P239 [style=Heading 2]: 3.3  Notebook and Spreadsheet Records
P240 [style=None]: Paper notebooks and spreadsheets are the most common record-keeping tools. They are cheap and flexible, and the North Pan case in this project shows that a field notebook can be a valuable source of pre-event readings. Their disadvantages concern consistency and provenance. Times are often approximate, units and sampling locations may be unstated, entries can be edited without a trace, and the relationship between an observation, a forecast and a decision is not captured in the structure of the record. When a value is later found to be unsupported, as happened with a hand-typed timestamp during this project, a simple spreadsheet has no mechanism to retain the displaced value and its claimed origin.
P241 [style=Heading 2]: 3.4  Weather Forecasts Used Alone
P242 [style=None]: Operators can obtain rainfall forecasts from meteorological services and weather applications. Forecasts are valuable and widely available, and they give advance notice. Their limitation, which motivates this project, is that a forecast describes the atmosphere and not the pond. Translating a forecast of rainfall in millimetres into a change in the salinity and depth of a particular pan requires the pan's state and a calculation. Forecasts also carry uncertainty in amount, timing and location, and a forecast issued before an event may differ from the rainfall that is later reported, as the March case in this project illustrates: the March event record carries a 40 mm event forecast and a reported actual rainfall of 90 mm.
P243 [style=Heading 2]: 3.5  Remote Sensing and Environmental Monitoring
P244 [style=None]: Remote sensing can monitor the wetting and drying of salt pans and the dynamics of surface crusts over large areas, as the studies in Chapter 2 show [7], [11]. The advantages are spatial coverage and the ability to build long records without field visits. The disadvantages, for pond-level management, are the revisit interval, sensitivity to cloud cover during rainfall events, limited ability to measure the salinity and depth of an individual shallow pan, and the fact that the output is a state map and not a decision. Satellite observation also does not record what the operator did.
P245 [style=Heading 2]: 3.6  Physics-Based and Hydrological Models
P246 [style=None]: A physics-based approach computes the effect of rainfall from a water and salt balance. At the simplest level the rainfall-only mass balance dilutes the brine in proportion to depth; richer models add evaporation, seepage, runoff, transfers and mixing. The advantages are transparency, reproducibility and the possibility of hand verification. A mass balance can be checked on paper, its assumptions can be written down, and its failures can be diagnosed.
P247 [style=None]: The disadvantages are that the model is only as good as its assumptions and inputs. A rainfall-only mass balance does not represent transfers, drainage, overflow, seepage, non-uniform mixing or measurement uncertainty. If an intervention has occurred and its volume is unknown, the model cannot be validated against the outcome. A more detailed model needs more inputs, and these inputs are often not recorded in routine salt-works practice.
P248 [style=Heading 2]: 3.7  Machine-Learning Predictors
P249 [style=None]: Machine-learning predictors have been applied to salinity in soils and rivers [6], [12], [15]. They can capture patterns that are difficult to write as equations, and they can combine many inputs. Their advantages depend entirely on the availability of a suitable labelled dataset. Their disadvantages in the salt-pan setting are the absence, at present, of such a dataset; the risk of leakage between training and test partitions when events from the same pan appear in both; the opacity of some models; and the danger that a numerical score is displayed to an operator before any independent evaluation has been done.
P250 [style=Heading 2]: 3.8  Generic Digital Twins and Decision-Support Platforms
P251 [style=None]: Digital-twin and decision-support platforms for agriculture and infrastructure [1], [8], [14] provide architectures in which sensor data, models and decisions are connected. Their advantage is integration. Their disadvantage for the present problem is generality: a generic platform does not by itself enforce a distinction between observed, forecast, simulated, recommended and decided information, does not by itself keep a history of corrections, and does not by itself prevent a model's output from being treated as a measurement.

P256 [style=Heading 2]: 3.10  Limitations That Motivate the Proposed Approach
P257 [style=None]: Four structural limitations recur across the techniques in Table 3.1. First, the evidentiary status of a value, whether it is observed, forecast or calculated, is usually implicit. Second, the relationship among an observation, a forecast, a calculation, a recommendation and a decision is not recorded. Third, corrections overwrite history, so a later reader cannot tell what was previously claimed. Fourth, field outcomes affected by interventions are reported as if they were clean evidence, or are discarded without trace. The proposed approach is designed around these four limitations.
P258 [style=Heading 2]: 3.11  How the Proposed Approach Addresses the Limitations
P259 [style=None]: SaltLens DSS does not replace the operator's judgement, remote sensing or a future machine-learning model. It provides a layer that holds the evidence and the decision together. Table 3.2 maps each limitation to the mechanism implemented in the system.

P262 [style=Heading 2]: 3.12  What the Proposed Approach Does Not Overcome
P263 [style=None]: It is important to be equally explicit about what the proposed approach does not do. It does not improve the accuracy of rainfall forecasts. It does not model evaporation, seepage, runoff, transfers or non-uniform mixing; the rainfall-only mass balance excludes them by design, and its output must be read as a reproducible counterfactual and not as a validated prediction. It does not provide a trained machine-learning model. It does not by itself make field measurements more accurate: an entered observation is not thereby independently verified. And it does not demonstrate that decisions made with its help are better than decisions made without it.
P264 [style=None]: These non-claims are part of the design, not an afterthought. The proposed approach overcomes the disadvantages of the existing techniques that concern traceability, evidence separation and auditability. It leaves the disadvantages that concern physical accuracy and operational benefit as explicit research boundaries, to be addressed by the studies described in Chapters 5 and 6.
P265 [style=Heading 2]: 3.13  Summary
P266 [style=None]: Existing techniques each offer something useful, from the operator's local knowledge to the transparency of a mass balance, but none of them alone provides a record that connects observations, forecasts, calculations, rules, decisions and field outcomes with their evidentiary status. The proposed approach targets that structural gap. The next chapter describes the system that implements it.

P307 [style=Heading 2]: 4.7  Rainfall-Only Mass Balance
P308 [style=Heading 3]: 4.7.1  Formulation
P309 [style=None]: The physical core of the system is a deterministic rainfall-only mass balance. Let C₀ denote the initial brine salinity in g/L, D₀ the initial water depth in cm and R the rainfall in cm. Under uniform mixing, constant dissolved salt mass and no other water flows, the dissolved salt per unit area is proportional to C₀D₀ before the rain and to C₁D₁ after it, where D₁ = D₀ + R is the depth after the rain. Equating the two gives the predicted post-rain salinity.
P310 [style=None]: 	C₁ = C₀ D₀ / (D₀ + R)	(4.1)
P311 [style=None]: 	D₁ = D₀ + R	(4.2)
P312 [style=None]: 	δ = 100 (1 − C₁ / C₀) %	(4.3)
P313 [style=None]: Here C₁ is the predicted post-rain salinity (g/L), D₁ the predicted depth (cm) and δ the predicted percentage dilution. Rainfall reported in millimetres is divided by ten before use. Substituting Equation (4.1) into Equation (4.3) shows that the dilution depends only on the ratio R/D₀:
P314 [style=None]: 	δ = 100 R / (D₀ + R) %	(4.4)
P315 [style=None]: The dilution is therefore independent of the initial salinity; a pan of any concentration loses the same fraction of its salinity for the same rainfall and initial depth. This is a useful sanity check on the implementation and is visible in Figure 4.5.
P316 [style=Heading 3]: 4.7.2  Volume and Salt-Mass Form
P317 [style=None]: The same balance can be written in terms of volume and mass, which is convenient for checking against a pan of known area A. The water volume is V = A D, and the dissolved salt mass is M = C V. Under the model assumptions the rainfall adds a volume ΔV = A R while the salt mass is unchanged:
P318 [style=None]: 	V₁ = A (D₀ + R),   M₁ = M₀ = C₀ A D₀	(4.5)
P319 [style=None]: For the North Pan, with a registered area of 1.23 ha (12,300 m²), a depth of 20 cm corresponds to a water volume of 12,300 × 0.20 = 2,460 m³, and, at 250 g/L (equivalently 250 kg/m³), a dissolved salt mass of 615,000 kg, that is 615 tonnes. These quantities are used in Chapter 5 to examine the reported March outcome.
P320 [style=Heading 3]: 4.7.3  Assumptions and Exclusions
P321 [style=None]: The mass balance is valid only under its stated assumptions. It assumes uniform mixing of rain and brine; it assumes constant dissolved salt mass; and it assumes that no other water flows occur. It therefore excludes transfer, drainage, overflow, seepage, non-uniform mixing and measurement uncertainty. It also excludes evaporation during the event, runoff into the pan, and the possibility that rain falls unevenly across the pan. These exclusions are not hidden: the interface states what the model leaves out, as shown in the September simulation screen of Figure 4.9, which explains that the calculation assumes complete mixing so that a single concentration describes the whole pan.
P322 [style=Heading 3]: 4.7.4  Worked Examples
P323 [style=None]: For the March scenario, R = 90 mm = 9 cm, C₀ = 250 g/L and D₀ = 20 cm. Then D₁ = 20 + 9 = 29 cm and C₁ = 250 × 20 / 29 = 172.414 g/L. The dilution is δ = 100 × (1 − 172.414/250) = 31.03 %. For the September scenario, R = 25 mm = 2.5 cm with the same initial state: D₁ = 22.5 cm, C₁ = 250 × 20 / 22.5 = 222.22 g/L and δ = 11.11 %. Both calculations can be reproduced with a pocket calculator, which is the point of choosing a deterministic mass balance.
P324 [style=Heading 3]: 4.7.5  Sensitivity of the Result
P325 [style=None]: Figure 4.4 shows the predicted salinity as a function of rainfall for several initial depths, with C₀ = 250 g/L. A shallower pan is diluted far more strongly by the same rainfall: at 10 cm initial depth, 90 mm of rain halves the salinity, whereas at 40 cm the same rain reduces it by about a fifth. This explains why shallow pans, which evaporate quickly and concentrate quickly, are also the most exposed to rainfall.

P332 [style=None]: def rainfall_only_balance(c0_g_l, d0_cm, rain_mm):
P333 [style=None]:     """Return post-rain salinity, depth and dilution (no-flow model)."""

P377 [style=Heading 2]: 4.9  Machine-Learning Evidence Gate
P378 [style=None]: The machine-learning component of SaltLens is an evidence gate and a deployment boundary, not a trained model. The gate checks whether genuine labelled training data and a validated deployed model exist. When they do not, it blocks predictive output, reports that ML prediction is unavailable, and ensures that no recommendation silently reads an ML estimate. In the audited state, the gate reports zero genuine labelled training rows, so no trained classifier or model-performance estimate is claimed.
P379 [style=None]: The reason for building a gate before a model is that a numerical score, once shown, tends to be treated as a result. The gate encodes the research policy that a score must not be shown before the evidence to support it exists. Consequently this report does not contain any ML accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, RMSE, MAE, calibration, risk score or SHAP result. The conditions under which such results could legitimately be produced are specified in Section 5.13.
P380 [style=Heading 2]: 4.10  Operator Decision Recording
P381 [style=None]: A recommendation is not a measured outcome, and an operator's acceptance of a recommendation is not a performed action. The system therefore records the operator's response as a separate decision with its own card. A decision has a disposition (accepted, modified or rejected), an actor, a time and a reason, and it is linked to the recommendation it answers. Figure 4.8 shows the decision recorded for the September demonstration: the recommendation was accepted for workflow testing and the record states that no physical harvesting was performed.
P383 [style=None]: Figure 4.8: Operator acceptance recorded for workflow testing; no physical harvest occurred
P384 [style=None]: The separation allows a future evaluation to compare recommendations, decisions and actions independently. It also allows the system to be evaluated when an operator deliberately rejects the suggestion, because the rejection is itself a recorded decision. Counting an acceptance as a harvest would overstate what the system has demonstrated, so the system requires a separate physical-action record before an action is treated as performed.
P385 [style=Heading 2]: 4.11  Field-Event Reconciliation
P386 [style=Heading 3]: 4.11.1  Purpose and Record Structure
P387 [style=None]: The field-validation module records rainfall events as structured, auditable records. A field event holds the event milestones, the pre-event and post-event measurements with their provenance, any interventions, the reported versus simulated results, the verification state and an audit history. The North Pan event links a distinct March pre-event observation rather than reusing a September observation, which prevents a different scenario's data from being attached to the event.
P388 [style=Heading 3]: 4.11.2  Timestamp Resolution
P389 [style=None]: During development, the March event's rainfall start was found to carry a timestamp of 14 March 2026 at 19:30 IST that had been typed by hand into a seed script, with no supporting rain-gauge record. The original North Pan field notebook, in contrast, gave the rainfall start as 14 March 2026 at 23:30 IST. The system provides a controlled timestamp-resolution workflow for such cases. The workflow adopts the supported value as the effective timestamp, retains the displaced value as superseded history together with its claimed origin, and prevents the operator-reported timestamp from being promoted to instrument verification. The event chronology is then marked reconciled, while the event and its measurements remain operator-reported and not independently verified.

P409 [style=Heading 3]: 4.11.3  Verification State
P410 [style=None]: The verification state of an event is recorded explicitly. In the March case, independently_verified is false: the pre-event observation is notebook-sourced, the post-event reading is operator-reported, and neither has been corroborated by an independent instrument or sheet. P411 [style=Heading 3]: 4.11.4  Interventions
P412 [style=None]: Interventions such as outbound brine transfers are recorded in the event with their timestamps and volumes. In the March event, an outbound transfer is recorded at 15 March 2026, 00:30 IST, but its volume is unknown. The module keeps such events visible and labelled, so that they can be excluded from quantitative validation rather than treated as zero error.
P413 [style=Heading 2]: 4.12  Exports
P414 [style=None]: Records can be exported for review. Exports carry UTC and IST representations of every timestamp, distinguish effective instants from superseded values, and preserve material caveats such as the unquantified transfer and the absence of independent verification. The corrected March export, for example, presents the notebook-supported rainfall start of 18:00 UTC (23:30 IST), the superseded 14:00 UTC (19:30 IST) value, and the event's operator-reported verification state. An export that dropped these caveats would allow the record to be misread, so the caveats are part of the export format.
P415 [style=Heading 2]: 4.13  Security, Authorisation and Integrity
P416 [style=None]: Because the system holds decisions and evidence, its operations are subject to authorisation checks and its records to integrity checks. The service tier enforces authorisation for operations that change records, and the software verification described in Section 4.16 includes checks of authorisation, audit integrity and exports. Audit history is append-only, so corrections add entries and do not overwrite earlier ones. Pan identity is retained with every record, so records cannot be silently reassigned from one pan to another.

P423 [style=Heading 2]: 4.15  Operator Workflow Walkthrough
P424 [style=None]: To show how the components fit together, this section walks through the sequence of operations an operator follows for one pan, using the September demonstration as the example. Each step is tied to the evidence category it produces.
P425 [style=List Paragraph]: Record an observation (OBSERVED). The operator opens the North Pan view and records a reading of 250 g/L salinity, 20 cm depth and 32.0 °C brine temperature. The form states that nothing entered is a figure until it is typed and that the entry declares its own provenance when saved. The reading is stored against pan_orginal with its UTC timestamp, source and observer.
P426 [style=List Paragraph]: Enter a weather forecast (FORECAST). The operator enters a 25 mm rainfall forecast with a 70 % probability and its issue time. The forecast is stored as a forecast and is never converted into an observation.
P427 [style=List Paragraph]: Review the digital twin (OBSERVED, with freshness). The twin is assembled from the recorded observation and forecast. Each contributing record is listed with its source and age, and the interface reports whether it is current.
P428 [style=List Paragraph]: Run the rainfall simulation (SIMULATED). The mass balance is run on the recorded state and the forecast rainfall. The screen reports 222.2 g/L and 11.1 % dilution and states what the model leaves out.
P429 [style=List Paragraph]: Review the recommendation (RECOMMENDED). The versioned rule is evaluated against the simulated value. The recommendation card shows the rule, its version, the compared variable, the value read, the threshold and the outcome, and states that no ML estimate was read.
P430 [style=List Paragraph]: Record the decision (DECIDED). The operator accepts, modifies or rejects the recommendation. The decision is stored on its own card with a reason. In the demonstration the acceptance was recorded for workflow testing, with an explicit note that no physical harvesting was performed.
P431 [style=List Paragraph]: File and reconcile the field event (reported outcome). After a real event, the operator or an auditor files a field event that links the pre-event observation, the rainfall milestones, any interventions and the post-event readings. Corrections to timestamps go through the controlled workflow, and the verification state is kept honest.
P432 [style=None]: The walkthrough illustrates the property on which the design depends: at every step, the record states what kind of thing it is. A reader who opens any record later can tell whether it is a reading, a forecast, a calculation, a rule outcome, a decision or a reported field outcome, and can follow the links back to its inputs.
P433 [style=Heading 2]: 4.16  Testing and Verification Approach
P434 [style=None]: Software verification in this project covers four things: that the equations are implemented as specified, that chronology constraints are respected, that authorisation, audit integrity and exports behave as specified, and that the machine-learning gate rejects a non-genuine dataset. Developer-reported tests and build checks support these checks. They demonstrate that a function rejects a non-genuine dataset, preserves an audit field, calculates a stated equation, or records a decision transition.
P435 [style=None]: It is equally important to state what the tests cannot show. They cannot show that a rainfall amount was measured correctly in the field, or that a threshold is universally appropriate. The reported test counts were obtained at different development checkpoints and are not represented as a single independently reproduced end-to-end run. The testing approach therefore supports the claim of computational validity, and it does not support claims of physical validity or operational benefit.
P436 [style=Heading 2]: 4.17  Summary
P437 [style=None]: This chapter has described a system in which evidence categories organise the whole design. The twin assembles source-labelled state; the mass balance computes a hand-verifiable counterfactual; the rule engine records why an action was suggested; the ML gate withholds unsupported predictions; operator decisions are recorded as decisions; and the field-event module records outcomes with their provenance and corrections. The next chapter presents the results obtained with this system and discusses what they do and do not establish.

P472 [style=None]: Rainfall of 90 mm on 12,300 m² delivers 1,107 m³ of water, which takes the pan from 2,460 m³ to 3,567 m³ in the no-flow model. A uniform reported depth of 50 cm would imply 6,150 m³, so the reported depth exceeds the no-flow value by 2,583 m³. The difference is not a measured inflow; it is the amount by which the reported depth, if it described the whole pan uniformly, differs from the no-flow calculation. The reported salinity of 20 g/L at 6,150 m³ would correspond to about 123 tonnes of dissolved salt, compared with 615 tonnes before the event, a difference of about 492 tonnes.
P473 [style=None]: These implied numbers show that the reported post-event state is not a small perturbation of the no-flow state. Both the water volume and the salt mass differ strongly from the no-flow expectation, and they differ in opposite directions in the sense that the reported depth is above the no-flow value while the reported salinity is far below it. Under the stated uniform-mixing reading, no outbound transfer alone can account for both, since removing brine would reduce depth. The numbers are therefore a reason for caution about interpreting the post-event readings, and not a conclusion about what happened.
P474 [style=Heading 2]: 5.6  Why the Difference Cannot Be Uniquely Attributed
P475 [style=None]: The reported discrepancy cannot be assigned uniquely to model error, transfer, freshwater inflow, spatial heterogeneity or instrument error. The possible contributors can be listed, but the present record cannot separate them.
P476 [style=List Paragraph]: Model error. The rainfall-only model excludes evaporation, seepage, runoff and non-uniform mixing. If the real pond experienced any of these, the no-flow result would differ from the outcome, but the size of the effect is unknown.
P477 [style=List Paragraph]: Unquantified transfer. An outbound brine transfer occurred, but its volume is unknown. Whether any other inflow or transfer occurred is not documented.
P478 [style=List Paragraph]: Additional freshwater inflow. Water from sources other than direct rainfall on the pan, such as runoff or inflow from neighbouring pans, could raise depth and lower salinity, but no record supports a specific inflow.
P479 [style=List Paragraph]: Spatial heterogeneity. A fresher surface layer or a measurement taken at a particular point can differ from the pan-wide average, so a point reading may not describe the whole pan.
P480 [style=List Paragraph]: Measurement or instrument error. The post-event readings are operator-reported, and their instrument, method and sampling location have not been independently documented.
P481 [style=None]: Because the contributors cannot be separated, the March case is classified as descriptive only. It exercises the complete reconciliation path, and it shows the system exposing a disagreement without converting it into a misleading accuracy claim, but it cannot establish rainfall-only model accuracy.

P501 [style=Heading 2]: 5.12  Threats to Validity
P502 [style=None]: Several threats to validity apply, and they are treated as explicit research boundaries and not as hidden assumptions.
P503 [style=List Paragraph]: Single field event. The study has one intervention-affected field event. It cannot support generalisation across rainfall regimes, initial conditions or pans.
P504 [style=List Paragraph]: Operator-reported measurements. Post-event salinity and depth are operator-reported and uncorroborated. Instrument, calibration and sampling location are not documented.
P505 [style=List Paragraph]: Unquantified intervention. The outbound transfer has unknown volume, so the post-event state cannot be interpreted as a rainfall-only response.
P506 [style=List Paragraph]: Idealised model. The mass balance excludes transfer, drainage, overflow, seepage and non-uniform mixing.
P507 [style=List Paragraph]: Scenario separation. The September scenario uses manually entered state and a configured rule. It must not be counted as a second validation event.
P508 [style=List Paragraph]: Configuration dependence. The 280 g/L threshold and comparison direction are configuration values requiring specialist review.
P509 [style=List Paragraph]: Software tests. Passing tests demonstrate software behaviour and cannot demonstrate field measurement accuracy.
P510 [style=List Paragraph]: Interface as evidence. Clear visual separation of evidence categories reduces ambiguity, but the interface does not create evidence absent from the underlying record.
P511 [style=Heading 2]: 5.13  Machine-Learning Evaluation Boundary
P512 [style=None]: The ML component is an evidence gate and deployment boundary, not a completed predictive model. The audited state contains no trained classifier, no validated ML experiment and no genuine salt-pan training set. Consequently accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, AUROC, RMSE, MAE, calibration, SHAP values, feature importance and other model-performance statistics are deliberately absent. Reporting a numerical ML score without a genuine labelled dataset would create a false impression of validation and would contradict the provenance policy implemented by the system.
P513 [style=None]: If a future field campaign supplies a sufficiently large and semantically consistent dataset, the ML task should first be defined in operational terms. For classification, the target must be an independently observed outcome, such as a documented intervention or risk category, and not a label inferred from the same recommendation rule that the model is supposed to replace. Training, validation and test splits should prevent records from the same event or pan, and future information, from leaking across partitions. Missing-value handling, scaling, feature selection and hyperparameter tuning must be confined to the training process, and the final held-out test set must remain untouched until model selection is complete.
P514 [style=None]: Only after those conditions are satisfied should predictive metrics be reported. Class imbalance should be reflected in the choice of metrics, and calibration should be checked if the model outputs probabilities used by a decision gate. For regression targets, MAE and RMSE should be accompanied by the sample count and a description of the measurement uncertainty. SHAP or another explanation method can then describe model associations, but it should not be interpreted as causal evidence.
P515 [style=Heading 2]: 5.14  Multi-Event Validation Protocol
P516 [style=None]: The March record is useful because it exercises the complete reconciliation path, but it cannot establish generalisation. A defensible multi-event study should register each rainfall event before the post-event result is inspected. Table 5.6 lists the information that should be retained for every event.
P517 [style=None]: Table 5.6: Information to retain for each event in a multi-event validation study
P519 [style=None]: With multiple independently verified events, the rainfall-only simulator can be evaluated using signed error, absolute error, MAE, RMSE, bias and residual analysis. Results should be stratified by rainfall magnitude, initial concentration, initial depth, pan and intervention status where the sample size permits. Repeated measurements from the same pan should not automatically be treated as independent sites, and a temporal or pan-level holdout should be retained for any later predictive model. These requirements define a future experiment; they are not presented as completed results.
P520 [style=Heading 2]: 5.15  Operational Benefit Boundary
P521 [style=None]: The present work does not demonstrate operational benefit. The September workflow records an operator acceptance for software testing, but acceptance of a recommendation is not evidence that a physical harvest occurred or that the selected action improved production. No controlled comparison of SaltLens-assisted decisions against ordinary operator practice, no measured change in salt recovery, no reduction in brine loss, and no reduction in decision time has been established.
P522 [style=None]: A future operational evaluation should define benefit measures before deployment. Candidate measures include decision time from forecast receipt to recorded action, frequency of avoidable transfers or drainage events, salt recovery or yield over comparable production periods, brine loss, number of decisions requiring manual reconciliation, and operator-reported workload. A controlled before/after or matched-period study would be preferable to isolated testimonials. Weather severity, pan state, operator experience and seasonal effects should be recorded because they can confound apparent benefit. The recommendation and the final physical action should remain separate records so that the system can be evaluated even when an operator rejects the suggested action.
P523 [style=None]: The demonstrated benefit of the prototype is therefore software-level traceability: an observation can be distinguished from a forecast, a calculation from a recommendation, and an operator decision from a physical outcome. That property supports reproducibility and safer future experimentation, but it must not be converted into a claim of improved salt production until field evidence demonstrates such an effect.
P524 [style=Heading 2]: 5.16  Claim-to-Evidence Discipline
P525 [style=None]: A central design requirement of SaltLens, and a principle of this report, is that each claim is linked to the evidence required to support it. Table 5.7 states the discipline.
P526 [style=None]: Table 5.7: Claim types and the evidence required to support them
P528 [style=None]: The same principle governs generalisation. More records alone are not sufficient if they lack comparable measurement semantics, if interventions are unrecorded, or if the same event appears in both development and evaluation data. A future dataset should preserve provenance at row and event level, document measurement methods and maintain event identifiers that permit leakage checks.
P529 [style=Heading 2]: 5.17  Summary
P530 [style=None]: The results show reproducible software behaviour and strong evidence control. The mass balance reproduces hand calculations; the March case exposes a large disagreement between a no-flow counterfactual and an intervention-affected field report without turning it into an accuracy claim; the September case demonstrates the rule-and-decision workflow; and the audit history preserves a corrected timestamp. The results do not establish generalised physical prediction, trained-ML performance or operational benefit. The final chapter summarises the conclusions and sets out the future work needed to establish those claims.
P531 [style=Heading 1]: CHAPTER 6
CONCLUSION AND FUTURE SCOPE
P532 [style=Heading 2]: 6.1  Conclusion
P533 [style=None]: This project set out to build a decision-support prototype for salt-pan rainfall management in which the evidentiary status of every figure and every decision is explicit. SaltLens DSS implements a provenance-labelled digital twin, a deterministic rainfall mass balance, versioned rule recommendations, recorded operator decisions and auditable field-event reconciliation, using a Next.js interface, FastAPI services and MongoDB Atlas storage.
P534 [style=None]: The March North Pan replay predicts 172.41 g/L and 29 cm from the reported 90 mm of rainfall, whereas the operator-reported outcomes of 20 g/L and 50 cm are materially different in an event that was affected by an outbound brine transfer of unknown volume. The separate September 25 mm forecast predicts 222.22 g/L and demonstrates the rule-and-decision workflow without documenting a physical harvest. The prototype therefore demonstrates reproducible software behaviour and evidence control. It does not establish generalised physical prediction, trained machine-learning performance or operational benefit. These three claims require separate empirical evidence and are intentionally left unclaimed.

P539 [style=List Paragraph]: The reported March post-event state implies a depth 2,583 m³ above the no-flow volume and a dissolved-salt mass about 492 tonnes below the pre-event value under a uniform-mixing reading, which is a reason for caution about interpreting the post-event readings.
P540 [style=List Paragraph]: The provenance workflow corrected an unsupported seed timestamp without erasing audit history, and exports carry the effective instant, the superseded value and the evidence limitations.
P541 [style=List Paragraph]: The September demonstration showed rule execution and a recorded operator acceptance, and exposed that the comparison direction, threshold and observation-freshness limit require specialist review.
P542 [style=List Paragraph]: The machine-learning gate reports zero genuine labelled training rows, so no ML performance is claimed.
P543 [style=Heading 2]: 6.3  Limitations
P544 [style=None]: The limitations of the work are the same as the evidence boundaries stated throughout this report. The field evidence is a single, operator-reported, intervention-affected event. The model is a rainfall-only no-flow balance. No machine-learning model exists. No controlled evaluation of operational benefit has been carried out. The rule threshold and direction are configuration values awaiting domain review. The software tests support implementation testing and not physical accuracy. These limitations do not reduce the value of the prototype as an auditable research platform, but they do determine what may be claimed from it.
P545 [style=Heading 2]: 6.4  Future Scope
P546 [style=Heading 3]: 6.4.1  Multi-Event Field Validation Campaign
P547 [style=None]: The most important next step is a field campaign that supplies multiple independently verified rainfall events, following the protocol in Section 5.14. Each event should be registered before its outcome is inspected; pre- and post-event salinity and depth should be measured with calibrated instruments at documented locations; rainfall should be recorded with a documented gauge; and every water-moving intervention should be logged with its volume and time. Events with missing intervention records should remain visible but be excluded from quantitative validation. Such a dataset would allow signed error, absolute error, MAE, RMSE and bias of the rainfall-only simulator to be reported with a sample count and measurement uncertainty.
P548 [style=Heading 3]: 6.4.2  Extended Physical Model
P549 [style=None]: The rainfall-only balance can be extended to include the flows it currently excludes. Let A be the pan area, E the evaporation depth over the period, and Q_in, Q_out, Q_drain and Q_seep the inflow, outbound transfer, drainage and seepage volumes, with salinities C_in, C_out, C_drain and C_seep. A more general no-crystallisation balance is:
P550 [style=None]: 	V₁ = V₀ + A R + Q_in − Q_out − Q_drain − Q_seep − A E	(6.1)
P551 [style=None]: 	M₁ = M₀ + C_in Q_in − C_out Q_out − C_drain Q_drain − C_seep Q_seep	(6.2)
P552 [style=None]: 	C₁ = M₁ / V₁	(6.3)
P553 [style=None]: With all flow terms and evaporation set to zero, Equation (6.1) to Equation (6.3) reduce to the rainfall-only balance of Equation (4.1) and Equation (4.2). The extended model has more inputs, and each must be recorded for the model to be validated; this is why the logging of interventions is a prerequisite and not an optional extra. Crystallisation of salt near saturation, and the non-uniform mixing of a fresher surface layer, would be further refinements that need dedicated measurements.
P554 [style=Heading 3]: 6.4.3  Instrumentation and Data Capture
P555 [style=None]: Integration with calibrated salinity and depth sensors and a documented rain gauge would reduce reliance on hand-recorded values and would raise the evidentiary status of observations, provided the system continues to record the instrument, calibration and sampling location as provenance. Mobile-friendly data entry, with offline capture and later synchronisation, would help in the field. A field data-capture template is given in Appendix C.
P556 [style=Heading 3]: 6.4.4  Machine-Learning Study
P557 [style=None]: If a sufficiently large and semantically consistent dataset is obtained, a machine-learning study can be undertaken under the conditions of Section 5.13. The target must be an independently observed outcome, splits must prevent leakage by event and by pan, and the final test set must remain untouched until model selection is complete. The evidence gate should then be released only when the dataset, the evaluation and the deployed model all satisfy the stated conditions, and predicted outputs should continue to be labelled ML-PREDICTED and kept apart from observed and simulated values.
P558 [style=Heading 3]: 6.4.5  Operational Evaluation
P559 [style=None]: A controlled before/after or matched-period study should compare assisted decisions with ordinary practice, using benefit measures defined before deployment: decision time, avoidable transfers or drainage events, salt recovery, brine loss, manual reconciliations and operator workload. Weather severity, pan state, operator experience and season should be recorded as potential confounders. A separate physical-action record should be introduced and used, so that recommendations, decisions and actions can each be analysed.
P560 [style=Heading 3]: 6.4.6  Rule Review and Decision-Support Enhancements
P561 [style=None]: The harvest and flood rule's threshold and comparison direction should be reviewed with salt-production specialists, and rules for other actions, such as pre-rain transfer, drainage and bund management, could be added as versioned configurations. An observation-freshness limit should be configured so that stale evidence is refused. Forecast uncertainty, including rainfall probability and the difference between forecast and reported rainfall, could be represented by running the mass balance over a range of rainfall values, as the sensitivity analysis of Chapter 4 suggests.
P562 [style=Heading 3]: 6.4.7  Deployment and Usability
P563 [style=None]: Before any operational deployment, the system should undergo a usability review with operators, an access-control and backup review of the storage tier, and a documented procedure for rule changes. Multi-pan and multi-site support would allow the pan-level audit to scale to a salt works with many pans.
P564 [style=Heading 2]: 6.5  Concluding Remarks
P565 [style=None]: SaltLens DSS should be regarded as an auditable research prototype and not as a validated production decision system. Its value is that it makes the difference between what was recorded, calculated, recommended, decided and verified visible and permanent. That property is a precondition for the later studies on physical accuracy, machine-learning performance and operational benefit, and it prevents a successful software demonstration from being mistaken for a completed scientific validation.

P594 [style=None]: The following worksheet records the steps for verifying a rainfall-only result by hand for the North Pan area of 12,300 m². The same sequence can be applied to any pan with known area, salinity and depth.
P595 [style=List Paragraph]: Record the pre-event salinity C₀ (g/L) and depth D₀ (cm) and the source and time of the reading.
P596 [style=List Paragraph]: Convert rainfall to centimetres: R (cm) = R (mm) / 10.
P597 [style=List Paragraph]: Compute the post-rain depth: D₁ = D₀ + R.
P598 [style=List Paragraph]: Compute the post-rain salinity: C₁ = C₀ D₀ / D₁.
P599 [style=List Paragraph]: Compute dilution: δ = 100 (1 − C₁ / C₀).
P600 [style=List Paragraph]: Cross-check with volumes: V₀ = A D₀ (with D₀ in metres), V₁ = A D₁, and confirm that C₀ V₀ = C₁ V₁.
P601 [style=List Paragraph]: State the exclusions: transfer, drainage, overflow, seepage, non-uniform mixing and measurement uncertainty.
