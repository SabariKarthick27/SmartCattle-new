SECTION: ABSTRACT (Starts at P[47], 3 paragraphs)
======================================================================
[48] The use of precision livestock health monitoring in smallholder dairy farms is frequently limited by the price of specific RFID readers, specific and proprietary wearable sensors, or fixed thresholds across a herd. These conventional approaches frequently trigger false alarms by failing to account for individual cattle physiological variations and sparse field data.
[49] To solve this problem, a platform called QR Based Cattle Management System is designed, which is an infrastructure-light cloud based cattle health monitoring system with the Composite Health Deviation Index (CHDI) to monitor the dairy health status based on personalized parameters of the herd. The system eliminates the need for costly hardware and makes use of weather-resistant QR passports that can be accessed with standard smart devices and backed by Role Based Access Control (RBAC). It contains a Three-Stage Adaptive CHDI engine at its core, which sets an individual baseline of 14-days, calculates multivariate Mahalanobis distance deviations, calculates 7-day temporal trend slopes, generates explainable feature attributions and applies an uncertainty gate to suppress false alarms during early enrollment.
[50] The application is a modern full-stack solution built with Next.js (App Router, Route Handlers), hosted on the Dockerized Neondb PostgreSQL database with Neon, using a dedicated FastAPI Python analytical microservice, and a responsive web application that provides real-time mobile QR scanning and veterinary triage workflows.System evaluation results show 90.48% sensitivity, 96.27% specificity, 62.30% precision and an F1 score of 73.79% in the detection of sub-clinical health 
anomalies leading to an advance clinical lead time of 2.4 days before the clinical symptoms.

SECTION: 1.1 Background of the Project (Starts at P[59], 1 paragraphs)
======================================================================
[60] Smallholder dairy farming is a key socio-economic pillar of more than 150 million rural farming households in global livestock production and rural economies. In India, livestock farming plays a crucial role in agriculture, supplying nutritional security and direct income to farmers daily. In spite of the importance of dairy herds from an economic perspective, pastoral farmers are regularly experiencing productivity issues due to the silent onset of sub-clinical disease, the delay in the time to veterinary diagnosis and the high costs of commercial Precision Livestock Farming (PLF) systems. The major economic losses associated with sub-clinical bovine mastitis, bovine respiratory disease (BRD) and metabolic ketosis include significant reductions in milk yield (25%–40% per lactation cycle), high veterinary expenses and early culling of high-value cows (Bowling et al. 2008; Kamphuis et al. 2010). Although modern electronic identification and wearable biosensors have been explored well, the commercial systems require expensive proprietary RFID tags, special reading wands and expensive wearable sensor collars. If you have less than 10 animals and spend thousands of dollars on proprietary hardware, it's simply not profitable. Moreover, current platforms use population-level fixed thresholds which do not take into account the natural variation in individuals, leading to excessive false alarms and farmer mistrust (Pereira et al. 2024; Kaur & Virk 2024). An infrastructure light, sensor optional, mathematically adaptive health monitoring solution for commodity mobile devices is urgently required.

======================================================================
SECTION: 1.2 Problem Statement (Starts at P[61], 4 paragraphs)
======================================================================
[62] Smallholder dairy producers and rural veterinary personnel frequently do not have access to affordable, accessible and reliable digital tools for livestock monitoring. There are three key issues that must be addressed for existing precision livestock systems to operate and economically benefit farming operations:
[63] 1.Expensive passive Low-Frequency (LF) or Ultra-High-Frequency (UHF) RFID ear tags and reader antennas are needed to use an existing cattle tracking system, which is not economically viable for smallholders without automated parlours. Standard monitoring platforms compare vital signs to static, global thresholds
[64] 2. Fixed Static Thresholds and Spurious False Alarms: Static thresholds are prone to false alarms as baseline body weight, milk yield, feed intake and temperature vary naturally in individual cows, different breeds and when cows are in different stages of lactation.
[65] 3. Rural farm data collection is often limited and sporadic, or environmental uncertainties are high. There are no uncertainty-aware abstention mechanisms in existing systems to avoid false alarms when registering new cows with limited baseline depth..

SECTION: 3.1 Existing Techniques (Starts at P[103], 1 paragraphs)
======================================================================
[104] Regular manual checkup by the farmers (eyes are dull, cud chewing is reduced, walking is changed, or udders are swollen) and occasional visits by veterinary officers are used in conventional livestock health management in smallholder dairy farming. Large commercial herds use automated monitoring based on passive RFID tags, walk-through antenna gates and proprietary collars (wearable items) that have fixed numerical thresholds.

======================================================================
SECTION: 3.2 Limitations of Existing Techniques (Starts at P[105], 5 paragraphs)
======================================================================
[106] Existing technologies have significant constraints and these constraints adversely impact the productivity of smallholders:
[107] Delayed Detection: When tissue damage and/or significant loss of milk occurs due to subclinical signs of mastitis and ketosis, treatment is less effective.
[108] High Capital Cost: Commercial RFID and IoT sensor collar systems have high capital cost, need to replace batteries frequently, and are locked into vendor systems.
[109] High Spurious Alarm Rates: Fixed population thresholds result in unnecessary spurious alarms when temperatures get hot during the day and during seasonal variations, leading to alarm fatigue.
[110] Broken Paper Records: Some veterinary history, vaccination dates and the time period for antibiotic use are documented on loose paper slips that carry a risk of antibiotic residues being present in commercial milk.

======================================================================
SECTION: 3.3 Proposed Technique (Starts at P[111], 1 paragraphs)
======================================================================
[112] The proposed technique introduces a QR-based cattle health management system an infrastructure-light, sensor-optional, cloud-native dairy health management system. The platform provides a safer, cheaper and more durable alternative to costly hardware, enabling the scanning of dynamic QR digital passports using any standard smartphone camera. An adaptive Three-Stage Composite Health Deviation Index (CHDI) engine assesses daily multi-signal health logs (daily milk yield, minutes in rumen, core body temperature, physical activity, feed intake, and body weight). CHDI calculates multivariate Mahalanobis distance anomalies, and implements a data-sufficiency abstention gate to reduce false alarms at the start of enrollment, while tracking personalized 14-day rolling baselines.

[116] Adaptive Anomaly Scoring: Calculate real-time CHDI scores (0-100), based on each cow's baseline.
[117] Dual-Tier Explainability: Provide simple explanations to farmers and technical explanations to vets using the Z-Scores.
[118] Closed-Loop Clinical Triage: Automate priority VetCase dispatch, diagnosis confirmation, treatment recording and active-learning classifier retraining.

[198] Stage 2: Multivariate Covariance & Mahalanobis CHDI Formulation:
[199] In Stage 2, the multivariate dependence across physiological systems is modeled by the regularized empirical covariance matrix Σ in Equation (4.4) because disordes in boviens impact multiple physiological systems at once (e.g., subclinical mastitis leads to a decline in both milk and feed intake and increased core body temperature).
[201] Where:
[202] • Σ(t) = 4 × 4 empirical covariance matrix capturing dependencies across the 4 core telemetry features (body weight, feed intake, milk production, core temperature).
[203] • zi(t − m) = 4-dimensional standardized deviation vector at observation step (t − m).
[204] • λreg · I = Tikhonov ridge shrinkage regularization (λreg = 10⁻³), guaranteeing positive-definite matrix inversion.
[206] In the multi-signal anomaly distance, the multivariate Mahalanobis distance in Equation (4.5) is calculated in a covariance-weighted space. This distance measures the multi dimensional deviation of the actual animal's vital measures from their own physiological centroid.
[208] Where:
[209] • DM(zi) = Multivariate Mahalanobis anomaly distance in covariance-weighted space.
[210] • zi = Standardized 4-signal deviation vector across all monitored features [zweight, zfeed, zmilk, ztemp]ᵀ.
[211] • Σ⁻¹ = Inverted regularized covariance matrix weighting correlated signals.
[213] The raw Mahalanobis distance DM is transformed to a bounded Composite Health Deviation Index (CHDI) on a 0-100 scale, by means of an exponential decay function as in Equation (4.6), for easy interpretation by farmers and clinicians.
[215] Where:
[216] • CHDI(zi) = Continuous health score bounded between 100 (Optimal Health) and 0 (Severe Critical Distress).
[217] • λ = 0.35 = Calibrated exponential decay scaling factor.
[218] • Clinical Bands: Healthy (≥ 75), Watch (50–74), At-Risk (< 50), and Abstain (N < 7 or Ct < 0.40).
[219] Stage 3: Temporal Trend, Explainability, and Uncertainty Estimation:
[220] The three algorithmic decision features in stage 3 include assessment of 7-day temporal trends, feature contribution and uncertainty gating.
[221] In order to gain insight into the downward trajectory momentum and to detect early insidious health degradation without breaching bounds, the 7-day evaluation of the rate of change is done by calculating the linear regression slope of the seven days of health scores in Equation (4.7).
[223] Where:
[224] • S = 7-day health trajectory slope evaluated across the last k = 7 consecutive CHDI scores.
[225] • t = Time step index (t ∈ {1, 2, ..., 7}) with midpoint t̄ = 4.
[226] • Clinical Rule: Negative slope (S < −2.0) indicates rapid health deterioration, escalating alert priority before static bounds are breached.
[228] Multi-signal alert departures are broken down by signal into their percentage contribution using the Leave-One-Covariate-Out (LOCO) permutation attribution in Equation (4.8).
[230] Where:
[231] • Ck = Percentage diagnostic contribution (%) of parameter k to the total anomaly alert.
[232] • DM(z) = Full Mahalanobis anomaly distance across all 4 telemetry features (body weight, feed intake, milk production, core temperature).
[233] • DM(z−k) = Counterfactual distance evaluated with variable k held at baseline.
[235] The data-sufficiency abstention gate is enforced to avoid spurious false alarms, particularly in the first few years of the cattle's registration when there is little history of observations using Equation (4.9).
[237] Where:
[238] • Conf(N) = Uncertainty confidence coefficient scaling between 0.0 and 1.0.
[239] • N = Count of consecutive valid daily observation logs recorded for the cow.
[240] • Nmin = 7 days = Minimum logging threshold required before issuing definitive clinical alerts, preventing false alarms on newly registered cows.

The resulting CHDI score (0-100) indicates a Healthy (80-100), Watch (60-79) or At-Risk (<60) status and specific clinical actions should be taken accordingly.

SECTION: 4.9 Data Preprocessing (Starts at P[254], 4 paragraphs)
======================================================================
[255] Cattle health analysis is conducted after a systematic validation and preprocessing process of raw field logs to ensure that the data is accurate, consistent and reliable.
[256] 1. Range Verification: All health measurements entered are compared to predetermined biological ranges. To ensure that data is not entered incorrectly and does not impact future calculations, Milk yields and body temperature are checked for implausibility before entering them into the form.
[257] 2. 14-Day Rolling Window Isolation: For each cow, the health observations made in the last 14 days (14 days before and after |W| = 14 days) are taken and are arranged chronologically. These records will be used to update individual baseline data such as the mean and variance of the physiological parameter, and allow the system to compare current physiological changes with the recent health history of the animal.
[258] 3. Regularized Inversion: Inversion of the covariance matrix is followed by regularization (Tikhonov ridge regularization) with regularization parameter λreg = 10⁻³. This procedure enhances the numerical stability, while also lowering the possibility of the system becoming singular if the health parameters are strongly correlated, which would hamper the calculation of Mahalanobis distance in multivariate anomaly detection.

[286] history = req.historical_observations
[287] current = req.current_observation
[288] num_points = len(history)
[289] # Stage 3: Data-Sufficiency Abstention Gate
[290] if num_points < req.min_history_days:
[291] return CHDIComputeResponse(
[292] cattle_id=req.cattle_id, score=None,
[293] confidence=round(min(1.0, num_points / float(N_SATURATION)), 3),
[294] status="abstain", triage_band="Abstain (Insufficient Baseline < 7 Days)")
[295] active_signals = ["weight", "feed", "milk", "temperature"]
[296] z_scores, means, stds, current_vec = {}, {}, {}, []
[297] for s in active_signals:
[298] vals = [getattr(h, s) for h in history if getattr(h, s, None) is not None]
[299] mean = float(np.mean(vals[-DEFAULT_WINDOW_SIZE:])) if vals else 0.0
[300] std = float(np.std(vals[-DEFAULT_WINDOW_SIZE:], ddof=1)) if len(vals) > 1 else 1.0
[301] z = (getattr(current, s) - mean) / (std + EPSILON)
[302] z_scores[s], means[s], stds[s] = float(z), mean, std
[303] current_vec.append(z)
[304] dim = len(current_vec)
[305] H = np.array([[getattr(h, s) for s in active_signals] for h in history], dtype=float)
[306] cov_reg = np.cov(H, rowvar=False) + TIKHONOV_LAMBDA * np.eye(dim)
[307] inv_cov = np.linalg.pinv(cov_reg)
[308] d_m = float(mahalanobis(np.array(current_vec), np.zeros(dim), inv_cov))
[309] score = max(0.0, min(100.0, round(100.0 * (1.0 - (d_m / D_MAX)), 1)))
[310] conf = round(float(min(1.0, num_points / float(N_SATURATION)) * max(0.0, 1.0 - (d_m / (3.0 * D_MAX)))), 3)
[311] status = "abstain" if conf < req.min_confidence else ("at-risk" if score < req.at_risk_below else ("watch" if score < req.watch_below else "healthy"))
[312] return CHDIComputeResponse(cattle_id=req.cattle_id, score=score, confidence=conf, status=status, raw_distance=round(d_m, 3))

SECTION: 5.3 Evaluation Metrics (Starts at P[406], 1 paragraphs)
======================================================================
[407] System evaluation follows standard statistical metrics:
• Sensitivity = TP / (TP + FN)
   Measures the proportion of true clinical disease anomalies successfully detected by the system.
•  Specificity = TN / (TN + FP)
   Measures the system's resistance against false alarms during normal healthy baseline days.
• Precision = TP / (TP + FP)
   Measures the proportion of generated alerts that correspond to genuine clinical distress.
•  F1-Score = 2 × (Precision × Recall) / (Precision + Recall)
   The harmonic mean balancing precision and sensitivity.
•  Lead Time = 2.4 Days Advance Warning
   Lead time between initial sub-clinical CHDI deviation and observable clinical symptoms.
