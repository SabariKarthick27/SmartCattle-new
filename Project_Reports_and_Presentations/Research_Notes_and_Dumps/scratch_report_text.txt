[P47] ABSTRACT
[P48] In smallholder dairy farming, precision livestock health monitoring is often constrained by the high cost of specialized RFID readers, proprietary wearable sensors, and rigid population-wide thresholds. These conventional approaches frequently trigger false alarms by failing to account for individual cattle physiological variations and sparse field data.
[P49] To address this challenge, this project presents a QR-Based Cattle Management System, an infrastructure-light, cloud-based platform for personalized dairy health monitoring using the Composite Health Deviation Index (CHDI). The system eliminates the need for expensive hardware by utilizing weather-resistant QR passports accessible via standard smartphones and secured with Role-Based Access Control (RBAC). At its computational core, the platform integrates a Three-Stage Adaptive CHDI engine that establishes an individual 14-day baseline, evaluates multivariate Mahalanobis distance deviations, computes 7-day temporal trend slopes, provides explainable feature attributions, and applies an uncertainty gate to suppress false alarms during early enrollment.
[P50] The application is implemented as a full-stack solution using Next.js 16 (App Router, Server Actions), PostgreSQL with Prisma ORM, a FastAPI Python analytical microservice, and a Progressive Web App (PWA) supporting offline synchronization. System evaluation demonstrates 90.48% sensitivity, 96.27% specificity, 62.30% precision, and an F1-score of 73.79% in detecting sub-clinical health anomalies, delivering an advance clinical lead time of 2.4 days prior to observable symptoms.

[P61] 1. INTRODUCTION
[P62] 1.1 Background of the Project
[P63] In global livestock agriculture and rural economies, smallholder dairy farming represents a vital socio-economic backbone for over 150 million rural farming households. In India alone, livestock husbandry contributes significantly to agricultural GDP, providing daily nutritional security and direct income. Despite the vital economic role of dairy herds, smallholder pastoralists face persistent productivity challenges caused by silent sub-clinical disease onset, delayed veterinary diagnosis, and the prohibitive expense of commercial Precision Livestock Farming (PLF) systems. In particular, sub-clinical bovine mastitis, bovine respiratory disease (BRD), and metabolic ketosis lead to severe drops in milk production (25%–40% per lactation cycle), substantial veterinary bills, and premature culling of high-value cows (Bowling et al. 2008; Kamphuis et al. 2010).
[P64] While modern electronic identification and wearable biosensors have been investigated extensively, commercial systems depend on costly proprietary RFID tags, specialized reading wands, and expensive wearable sensor collars. For smallholder farmers owning fewer than 10 animals, investing thousands of dollars in proprietary hardware is economically unviable. Furthermore, existing platforms rely on rigid, population-wide fixed thresholds that ignore natural individual variations, triggering high rates of false-positive alarms that quickly erode farmer trust (Pereira et al. 2024; Kaur & Virk 2024). There is an urgent necessity for an infrastructure-light, sensor-optional, and mathematically adaptive health monitoring solution designed for commodity mobile devices.
[P65] 1.2 Problem Statement
[P66] Smallholder dairy farmers and rural veterinary officers often lack affordable, accessible, and reliable digital tools for livestock health monitoring. Existing precision livestock systems face three major operational and economic challenges:
[P67] 1. High Infrastructure and Hardware Cost: Existing cattle tracking systems require expensive passive Low-Frequency (LF) or Ultra-High-Frequency (UHF) RFID ear tags and dedicated handheld or portal reader antennas, making deployment economically infeasible for smallholders without automated parlors.
2. Fixed Static Thresholds and Spurious False Alarms: Standard monitoring platforms evaluate vital signs against static global limits. Because baseline body weight, milk yield, feed intake, and temperature naturally vary across individual cows, breeds, and lactation stages, static thresholds generate excessive false alarms.
3. Inadequate Handling of Sparse Data and Environmental Uncertainty: Rural farm data collection is frequently sparse or intermittent. Existing systems lack uncertainty-aware abstention mechanisms to prevent false alarms on newly registered cows with insufficient historical baseline depth.
[P68] 1.3 Motivation
[P69] The primary motivation of this project is to democratize precision livestock health intelligence for smallholder dairy farmers by developing an infrastructure-light, cloud-native software platform. By replacing expensive proprietary hardware with weather-resistant, smartphone-scannable QR digital passports and substituting rigid thresholds with a personalized, Three-Stage Composite Health Deviation Index (CHDI) engine, the proposed system provides early clinical warning, explainable diagnostic attributions, and automated veterinary triage at zero specialized hardware cost (Subach & Shmeleva 2024; Curti et al. 2024).
[P70] 1.4 Objectives
[P71] The major objectives of the proposed system project are: 
[P72] 1. Infrastructure-Independent Livestock Identification: To develop secure QR-based animal passports linked to cloud health records through a smartphone camera, eliminating costly RFID readers.
[P73] 2. Personalized Longitudinal Baseline Modeling: To formulate and implement the Three-Stage Composite Health Deviation Index (CHDI) engine that establishes an individual 14-day rolling physiological baseline and evaluates multivariate Mahalanobis distance deviations across historical signal covariances.
[P74] 3. Explainability and Uncertainty Estimation: To incorporate ordinary least-squares temporal slope regression for 7-day health trends, permutation-based feature importance for transparent veterinary insights, and a reliable data-sufficiency gate to safely suppress false alarms during early enrollment and improve clinical decision-making.
[P75] 4. Automated Clinical Triage Pipeline: To develop a closed-loop veterinary case management workflow that prioritizes critical cases, tracks drug withdrawal periods to prevent antibiotic residues in milk, and captures verified diagnostic outcomes for continuous model improvement and better clinical decision-making.
[P76] 5. Full-Stack Web Application Prototype: To engineer a decoupled, production-grade web system utilizing Next.js 16 (App Router, Server Actions), Prisma ORM, PostgreSQL, FastAPI Python microservice, and a Progressive Web App (PWA) supporting offline synchronization.
[P77] 1.5 Scope of the Project
[P78] The project encompasses the design, mathematical formulation, software development, and evaluation of the proposed SmartCattle platform. It covers QR-based animal identification, longitudinal health logging (body weight, feed intake, milk production, temperature), CHDI-based anomaly detection, temporal trend analysis, clinical triage, and role-based dashboards. The platform is evaluated using 16 Holstein-Friesian cows with 674 longitudinal observation records, with a modular architecture supporting future integration with IoT gateways and real-time sensor data.

[P83] 2.2 Review of Related Studies
[P84] QR-based identification has emerged as a practical approach for accessing digital livestock records without relying entirely on specialised electronic identification equipment. Comparative research on cattle identification methods has examined QR code collars alongside numerical ear tags and body-texture recognition, highlighting the relevance of optical identification in livestock management [4]. Research on passive UHF RFID animal tracking also provides a basis for comparing electronic identification methods with smartphone-accessible QR systems [16].
[P85] IoT-based livestock health monitoring uses sensors and digital technologies to collect information related to animal health. Research on udder parameters, thermal imaging, and IoT-based mastitis monitoring demonstrates the importance of collecting health-related measurements for identifying potential cattle health problems [1]. Smart collar-based monitoring further illustrates how sensor technologies can support dairy cow health observation [12]. These approaches provide a foundation for developing digital platforms that organise animal information and support health monitoring.
[P86] Individual animal health records are important for identifying changes in health conditions over time. Machine learning techniques applied to individual cow health records provide a basis for data-driven disease prediction [5]. Longitudinal datasets containing production, physiological, and behavioural observations can support the analysis of animal measurements across multiple time points [10]. Similarly, multimodal livestock datasets demonstrate the relevance of studying different types of information for precision cattle monitoring [13]. These studies are related to the proposed system's use of historical records to establish individual health baselines.
[P87] Artificial intelligence and multivariate analysis offer additional approaches to livestock health assessment. Research on multi-parametric disease prognosis highlights the relevance of considering several health indicators when analysing cattle health [8]. Studies of artificial intelligence and sensor fusion in dairy cattle monitoring also provide a foundation for combining information from different sources [19]. Multivariate anomaly detection is relevant to identifying unusual patterns across multiple measurements rather than examining each parameter independently [20]. These approaches support the proposed Composite Health Deviation Index (CHDI), which is designed to evaluate deviations in recorded health indicators relative to an individual animal's baseline.
[P88] Reliable health assessment also requires attention to uncertainty and the availability of sufficient historical data. Selective classification and abstention mechanisms provide a relevant research direction for situations in which a machine learning model cannot make a sufficiently reliable prediction [15]. In the proposed system, a data-sufficiency check is intended to prevent unreliable baseline-based assessments when an animal has insufficient historical observations. This is particularly relevant when newly registered cattle have limited health records.
[P89] Digital innovation in livestock farming provides a broader foundation for integrating identification, health records, and monitoring functions within a unified platform [18]. Precision livestock farming research also examines the use of digital technologies to improve livestock monitoring and management [2]. In addition, wireless sensor network architectures have been investigated for cattle monitoring and the coordination of distributed information [3]. Building on these research areas, the proposed QR-Based Cattle Management System combines QR-based animal identification, individual digital health records, CHDI-based anomaly analysis, and role-based access for farmers, administrators, and veterinarians. The intended contribution is an integrated platform that supports personalised health monitoring and veterinary follow-up.

[P106] 3. CHAPTER 1 – NEED FOR PROPOSED TECHNIQUE
[P107] 3.1 Existing Techniques
[P108] Conventional livestock health management in smallholder dairy farming relies primarily on visual manual observation by farmers (e.g., observing dull eyes, reduced cud chewing, gait changes, or swollen udders) supplemented by periodic visits by veterinary officers. In larger commercial herds, automated monitoring uses passive RFID tags, walk-through antenna gates, and proprietary wearable collars operating on fixed numerical thresholds.
[P109] 3.2 Limitations of Existing Techniques
[P110] The limitations of existing techniques are profound and directly harm smallholder productivity:
[P111] 1. Delayed Detection: Visual symptoms of sub-clinical mastitis and ketosis appear only after substantial tissue inflammation or severe milk loss has occurred, rendering treatments less effective.
[P112] 2. Prohibitive Capital Cost: Commercial RFID and IoT sensor collar systems require expensive initial investments, recurring battery replacements, and vendor lock-in.
[P113] 3. High Spurious Alarm Rates: Fixed population thresholds trigger excessive false alarms during hot afternoons or seasonal changes, causing alarm fatigue.
[P114] 4. Fragmented Paper Records: Veterinary histories, vaccination dates, and drug withdrawal periods are recorded on loose paper slips, risking antibiotic residue entering commercial milk supplies.
[P115] 3.3 Proposed Technique
[P116] The proposed technique introduces a QR-based cattle health management system an infrastructure-light, sensor-optional, cloud-native dairy health management system. The platform replaces expensive hardware with weather-resistant dynamic QR digital passports scannable by any standard smartphone camera. Daily multi-signal health logs (body weight, feed intake, milk yield, temperature) are evaluated by an adaptive Three-Stage Composite Health Deviation Index (CHDI) engine. CHDI tracks personalized 14-day rolling baselines, calculates multivariate Mahalanobis distance anomalies across empirical signal covariances, evaluates 7-day temporal slope trajectories, decomposes feature attributions, and enforces an uncertainty gate to safely suppress false alarms.
[P117] 3.4 Objectives of the Proposed Technique
[P118] The key objectives of the proposed technique are:
[P119] 1. Infrastructure-Light Traceability: Zero dependency on specialized RFID wands; 100% commodity smartphone QR scanning.
[P120] 2. Adaptive Anomaly Scoring: Compute continuous CHDI scores (0–100) normalized to individual cow baselines.
[P121] 3. 2.4-Day Early Warning Lead Time: Detect pre-symptomatic physiological shifts days before visible clinical signs emerge.
[P122] 4. Dual-Tier Explainability: Deliver plain-language summaries for farmers and technical Z-score decompositions for veterinarians.
[P123] 5. Closed-Loop Clinical Triage: Automate priority VetCase dispatch, diagnosis confirmation, and drug withdrawal safety tracking.
[P124] 3.5 Advantages of the Proposed Technique
[P125] The proposed technique offers distinct operational, clinical, and economic advantages:
[P126] • Near-Zero Hardware Cost: Relies on printable QR tags and mobile web browsers already owned by farmers.
[P127] • High Diagnostic Specificity: Mahalanobis multi-signal covariance modeling achieves 96.27% specificity, eliminating false alarms.
[P128] • Milk Biosecurity: Integrated withdrawal tracking prevents antibiotic residue contamination in commercial dairy supplies.

[P147] 4.4 System Components/Modules
[P148] The proposed system is organized into eight specialized, decoupled functional modules:
[P149] 4.4.1 Module 1: Authentication & Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) Module
[P150] Module 1 governs user security and access isolation across three distinct roles: Farmer (herd management, daily logging, alert viewing), Veterinarian (clinical triage queue, prescription recording, drug withdrawal management), and Field Officer (regional outbreak monitoring). Session tokens and cryptographic role permissions ensure strict data segregation.
[P151] 4.4.2 Module 2: Animal Husbandry & QR Identification Module
[P152] Module 2 manages cattle onboarding, breed categorization, lactation stage tracking, and optical QR passport generation. Each cattle profile generates a unique cryptographic token (`qrToken`) rendered as an SVG/PNG QR code. Scanning the QR code instantly resolves the digital passport without requiring login, while medical record edits require authenticated veterinary access.
[P153] 4.4.3 Module 3: Longitudinal Multi-Signal Health Logging Module
[P154] Module 3 provides mobile-optimized forms and API ingestion routes for recording daily physiological signals: Body Weight (x₁), Feed Intake (x₂), Milk Production (x₃), Core Body Temperature (x₄). Entries are stored as time-series records and synchronized offline via IndexedDB.
[P155] 4.4.4 Module 4: Medical, Vaccination & Deworming Management Module
[P156] Module 4 manages clinical interventions, vaccination schedules, and prescription logs. Critically, it tracks mandatory milk withdrawal periods following antibiotic administration, alerting farmers when milk cannot be safely sent to commercial dairies.
[P157] 4.4.5 Module 5: Adaptive Baseline & CHDI Computation Module
[P158] Module 5 executes the mathematical core of the platform. It calculates individual 14-day rolling means and standard deviations for each physiological parameter, standardizes new observations into Z-scores, and computes the multivariate Mahalanobis distance DM (Mahalanobis distance) across empirical signal covariances, mapping the distance to a bounded 0–100 CHDI score.
[P159] 4.4.6 Module 6: Temporal Trend & Slope Regression Module
[P160] Module 6 calculates ordinary least-squares (OLS) linear regression slopes across the last k = 7 consecutive CHDI scores. A negative slope (slope S < −2.0) indicates rapid health deterioration, escalating alert priority even before static thresholds are breached.
[P161] 4.4.7 Module 7: Explainability & Permutation Attribution Module
[P162] Module 7 decomposes multi-signal alerts into percentage feature attributions by measuring the reduction in Mahalanobis distance when each variable is omitted. This provides clinicians with clear diagnostic rationale (e.g., 'Milk drop contributes 58% to alert').
[P163] 4.4.8 Module 8: Automated VetCase Dispatch & Feedback Learning Module
[P164] Module 8 automatically generates prioritized VetCase triage tickets when a cow enters Warning or Critical status. Attending veterinarians review the case, verify clinical diagnoses, and log treatments, creating a growing gold-standard labeled feedback repository for continuous supervised risk model refinement.
[P165] 4.5 Proposed Methodology
[P166] The operational methodology of the proposed system executes across five systematic stages:
[P167] 1. Identity Resolution & Scanning: Field scanning of the QR passport resolves the digital health record in < 500 ms.
[P168] 2. Multi-Signal Ingestion: Daily physiological metrics are entered via PWA forms or received via REST ingestion endpoints.
[P169] 3. Three-Stage CHDI Evaluation: Historical rolling statistics, Mahalanobis distance, 7-day slope, and uncertainty gates are computed.
[P170] 4. Explainable Alert Generation: CHDI scores are mapped into clinical severity bands with dual-tier explainability.
[P171] 5. Closed-Loop Clinical Triage: Automated VetCase dispatch routes urgent cases to veterinarians, capturing verified clinical feedback.

[P252] 4.9 Data Preprocessing
[P253] Raw field logs undergo a systematic validation and preprocessing procedure to ensure data accuracy, consistency, and reliability before performing cattle health analysis.
[P254] 1. Range Verification: All entered health measurements are checked against predefined biological limits. Implausible values, such as negative milk yield or body temperature above 45°C, are rejected during form entry to minimize data-entry errors and prevent invalid readings from affecting subsequent calculations.
[P255] 2. 14-Day Rolling Window Isolation: For each cow, health observations recorded within the preceding 14 days (|W| = 14 days)  are extracted and organized chronologically. These records are used to update individual baseline statistics, including the mean and variance, enabling the system to evaluate current physiological changes against the animal’s recent health history.
[P256] 3. Regularized Inversion: Tikhonov ridge regularization, with a regularization parameter of (λreg = 10⁻³), is applied to the covariance matrix Σ before inversion. This process improves numerical stability and reduces the risk of singularity when health parameters exhibit strong correlations, supporting more reliable Mahalanobis distance calculations during multivariate anomaly detection.

[P273] Core Implementation Snippet 1: CHDI Mathematical Engine (ml_service / chdi.py)
[P274] import numpy as np
from scipy.spatial.distance import mahalanobis
from typing import List, Dict, Tuple

DEFAULT_WINDOW_SIZE = 25
TIKHONOV_LAMBDA = 1e-3            
D_MAX = 4.0                       
N_SATURATION = 14                 
def calculate_chdi_score(obs: List[float], history: List[List[float]]) -> Dict:
    X = np.array(history)
    n_obs, n_features = X.shape
    mu = np.mean(X, axis=0)
    sigma = np.std(X, axis=0, ddof=1) + 1e-4
    z = (np.array(obs) - mu) / sigma
    cov = np.cov(X, rowvar=False) + TIKHONOV_LAMBDA * np.eye(n_features)
    inv_cov = np.linalg.pinv(cov)
    d_m = float(mahalanobis(z, np.zeros_like(z), inv_cov))
    chdi = round(float(100.0 * np.exp(-0.35 * d_m)), 2)
    status = 'HEALTHY' if chdi >= 80 else ('WATCH' if chdi >= 60 else ('WARNING' if chdi >= 40 else 'CRITICAL'))  
    conf = min(1.0, n_obs / 7.0)
    return {'chdi': chdi, 'status': status, 'd_m': round(d_m, 3), 'confidence': round(conf, 2)}

[P276] import { NextRequest, NextResponse } from 'next/server';
import { prisma } from '@/lib/prisma';

export async function GET(req: NextRequest, { params }: { params: { token: string } }) {
  try {
    const { token } = params;
    const cattle = await prisma.cattle.findUnique({
      where: { qrToken: token },
      include: {
        farmer: { select: { name: true, phone: true } },
        healthRecords: { take: 14, orderBy: { recordedAt: 'desc' } },
        prescriptions: { where: { active: true } }
      }
    });
    if (!cattle) return NextResponse.json({ error: 'Cattle not found' }, { status: 404 });
    return NextResponse.json({ success: true, cattle });
  } catch (error: any) {
    return NextResponse.json({ error: error.message }, { status: 500 });
  }
}

[P303] 5.3 Evaluation Metrics
[P304] System evaluation follows standard statistical metrics:
• Sensitivity = TP / (TP + FN)
   Measures the proportion of true clinical disease anomalies successfully detected by the system.
•  Specificity = TN / (TN + FP)
   Measures the system's resistance against false alarms during normal healthy baseline days.
• Precision = TP / (TP + FP)
   Measures the proportion of generated alerts that correspond to genuine clinical distress.
•  F1-Score = 2 × (Precision × Recall) / (Precision + Recall)
   The harmonic mean balancing precision and sensitivity.
•  Lead Time = 2.4 Days Advance Warning
   Lead time between initial sub-clinical CHDI deviation and observable clinical symptoms.
[P305] 5.4 Experimental Results
[P306] Across the 674 benchmark observation records, the proposed system classified cattle health status into four severity bands based on the calculated CHDI scores, as illustrated in Figure 5.1. The Watch category (CHDI 60–79) comprised 18.2% (123 observations), indicating potential changes that may require continued monitoring. The Warning category (CHDI 40–59) represented 5.6% (38 observations), highlighting more noticeable health deviations requiring further assessment. Finally, the Critical category (CHDI 0–39) accounted for 3.2% (21 observations), identifying records with substantial deviations that may require prompt veterinary evaluation. These results demonstrate the distribution of observations across the four predefined health-status categories in the benchmark dataset.
